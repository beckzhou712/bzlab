Building a Retrieval Benchmark on NYC Open Data

23 Aug, 2026
|Edit page

Three-sentence overview. Given a fixed pool of datasets (here, NYC Open Data), the hard part of building a benchmark is never the pool itself but the other two ingredients — the queries and the graded relevance judgments (qrels) — and there are two mature, complementary technical routes for producing them: the twenty-year TREC/NTCIR tradition of human-written queries plus human graded judgments (with a whole methodology for computing NDCG under incomplete judgment pools), and the post-2022 surge of using large language models to generate queries and to act as relevance assessors that produce synthetic qrels. The current consensus is that LLMs generate usable queries and, for comparing systems’ relative ranking, produce qrels that agree closely with human labels — but they cannot yet fully replace human assessment, so the safest deployable design is a hybrid pipeline of “large-scale LLM generation plus stratified human verification.”

This is a focused review (~36 core papers) serving one engineering goal: construct an NDCG-evaluable dataset-retrieval benchmark on NYC Open Data. The first of the three benchmark ingredients — the dataset pool comes from NYC Open Data — so this review spends all of its length on the other two: how to produce, scientifically and reproducibly, the queries and the graded qrels.

Landscape: how the two routes evolved

Dataset retrieval (dataset search) became a recognized IR sub-task surprisingly late, and its “test collection” consciousness later still. As dataset-retrieval methods moved from metadata-based to content-based, the field long lacked a dedicated test collection — Lin et al. 2022 (ACORDAR) built the first test collection for ad hoc content-based dataset retrieval, where both the queries and the relevance judgments were annotated by human experts using a dashboard purpose-built for browsing a dataset’s metadata and its data content before scoring. Earlier, Kato et al. 2021 (NTCIR Data Search) had built a collection from the US Data.gov and Japanese e-Stat government open-data portals, with human-written English/Japanese topics and relevance judgments over the pool returned by 74 retrieval systems — the closest precedent to the NYC Open Data setting, because its pool is likewise a government open-data portal.

The figure below groups this review’s 36 papers by which technical angle they use to construct queries and qrels, with year on the x-axis and citations (log) on the y-axis. Two time strata are visible: 2006–2018 is the methodological foundation era (evaluation metrics under incomplete judgments, query-log analysis), and post-2022 is the LLM ignition era (synthetic queries and synthetic judgments taking off together).

Of the five routes, R1 (dataset-retrieval test collections themselves) and R2 (human-query-and-judgment methodology) form the skeleton of Route One; R3 (LLM synthetic queries) and R4 (LLM relevance judging) form Route Two; R5 (dataset-retrieval methods) is supporting upstream/downstream work. Below, I first explain why qrels are the hardest link, then walk each route with operational detail.

Why qrels are the most expensive, hardest link in a benchmark

Queries are only “what a user would type” — they can come from logs, human authors, or an LLM, at manageable cost. What is genuinely expensive is the qrels: for each query, judging which datasets in the pool are relevant and how relevant (a graded score). Without qrels there is no way to compare a system’s ranking against the correct answer, and no way to compute NDCG. And qrels are costly in two layers. First, judgment volume — in principle every query requires judging the entire pool, and NYC Open Data has thousands of datasets, so exhaustive judgment is infeasible. Second, judgment difficulty — deciding whether a dataset satisfies a query often requires reading the title, description, field schema, and data samples together, which is harder than judging a document. IR developed the pooling + robust-metrics-under-incomplete-judgments methodology for the first layer (Route One’s R2), and recently pins hopes on LLMs for the second (Route Two’s R4). Understanding both layers is a prerequisite for designing the NYC benchmark.

Route One: human-authored queries and human graded judgments

The core idea of this route is to let real people produce the queries and let trained assessors produce the graded qrels, controlling cost and bias with the TREC/NTCIR evaluation methodology accumulated over decades. Its credibility is the highest; the cost is human labor. For the specific setting of government open data, there are already reusable resources and protocols.

Ready-made human-annotated collections (reuse or adapt directly)

The most valuable point for you: test collections that are pool-isomorphic to yours and already carry human queries and human graded judgments exist, so you may not need to annotate from scratch. Kato et al. 2021’s NTCIR Data Search collection is built directly on US Data.gov and Japanese e-Stat, with human-written topics and graded relevance judgments pooled over 74 systems — the most direct template for stitching together “government open data + human queries + human qrels.” Its topic-writing guidelines, grade-level definitions, and pooling depth can be transplanted to NYC Open Data. The dataset-retrieval test-collection line was then carried on by Lin et al. 2022 (ACORDAR): as the first content-based dataset-retrieval test collection, its key contribution is the expert annotation dashboard that lets assessors browse metadata and data content before scoring — the lesson that “assessors need to see the data itself, not just title and description” is critical for the NYC setting.

ACORDAR’s follow-ups directly expose and repair two systematic flaws of human test collections, and make a useful “pitfall checklist” for your annotation design. Chen et al. 2024 (ACORDAR 2.0) points out the lexical bias in pooling: building the judgment pool from keyword retrieval alone misses datasets that are semantically relevant but lexically different, biasing qrels toward lexical-match systems; their fix introduces dense retrieval models to enlarge the pool. This maps straight onto the NYC setting — if you select “which datasets to send for human judgment” using only BM25-style lexical retrieval, the final benchmark will underrate semantic retrievers. More recently, Shi et al. 2025 (DSEBench) generalizes the task to “query + example dataset” joint retrieval and asks for the relevant fields to be identified, indicating that relevance judgment in dataset search is refining from “whole-dataset relevant/not” toward “field-level relevance.” Separately, Viswanathan et al. 2023 (DataFinder) recommends datasets from a natural-language description of a research need; its “turn a one-sentence need into retrieval constraints” idea is a query source that sits between fully manual and fully automatic.

Anchoring “what users actually type” with real query logs

The biggest risk of human-written queries is drifting away from real user behavior — queries a researcher invents at a desk may look nothing like what citizens actually type into an open-data portal. Kacprzak et al. 2018 analyzed real portal search logs and data requests and systematically characterized how “dataset search queries” differ from ordinary web-search queries (shorter, skewed toward place/agency/topic terms, often carrying temporal and geographic qualifiers) — precisely the empirical basis for queries like “NYC taxi data.” For NYC Open Data, the most scientific query source is the portal’s own search or API-access logs, if obtainable; if not, Kacprzak’s query-feature distribution can serve as a template and quota constraint for human-written queries (e.g., allocate query types by the real proportions of “topic term / agency name / geographic qualifier / temporal qualifier”).

Computing reliable NDCG when judgments are incomplete

The real methodological core of Route One is a body of work that keeps evaluation reliable when exhaustive judgment is impossible — a hard requirement for a pool of thousands of NYC datasets. Carterette et al. 2006 (minimal test collections) shows that by actively selecting only the few documents that best discriminate between systems, one obtains a system ranking almost identical to full judgment; Yilmaz and Aslam 2006 (statAP) gives statistical methods to estimate average precision under incomplete and sampled judgments; Sakai 2008 systematically compares metrics such as bpref designed for incomplete judgments, showing how their robustness differs as pools grow shallow. Together these answer a question you will certainly hit: the NYC benchmark cannot judge every dataset for every query — so how many to judge, how, and with what metric, so that NDCG rankings stay trustworthy? The standard practice is pooling: take the union of each of several different retrieval systems’ top-k as the judgment pool, judge (grade) only that pool by hand, and treat everything outside it as non-relevant. ACORDAR 2.0’s lesson: mix semantic/dense retrievers into the pool, or it will carry a lexical bias.

Route Two-A: generating queries with LLMs

This route’s core idea is to have an LLM read a document (or dataset) and generate the queries for which that document would be relevant — which simultaneously produces a (query, relevant-item) pair, i.e., a weak positive qrel. It originated in the neural-retrieval training-data problem, but the same machinery transfers directly to benchmark construction.

The foundational technique is document-to-query generation. Nogueira et al.’s doc2query line (here via the “doc2query—” analysis) trains a model to predict the queries a document answers; the retrieval-benchmark descendants make this few-shot and LLM-driven. Bonifacio et al. 2022 (InPars) uses the few-shot ability of large LMs to generate, from a handful of examples, synthetic (query, document) training pairs for a target domain; Dai et al. 2022 (Promptagator) makes the case sharpest — “few-shot dense retrieval from 8 examples”: each retrieval task comes with a short description and a few examples, and the LLM amplifies them into a large synthetic training set tailored to that task’s search intent. For your purpose, the transferable recipe is: write ~8 seed (dataset → plausible query) examples in the NYC style, then have the LLM mass-generate queries conditioned on each dataset’s metadata and a data sample. Wang et al. 2022 (GPL) adds the crucial refinement of generative pseudo-labeling: it doesn’t trust the generated query blindly, but re-scores each (query, document) pair with a cross-encoder to attach a soft relevance label — a design that directly anticipates graded (rather than binary) qrels.

Two papers speak directly to using generated queries for test collections rather than for training. Alaofi et al. 2023 explores generating queries and query variants from a description of an information need and quantifies how similar LLM-generated queries are to human ones, finding real promise in generating variants — while cautioning that LLMs do not fully capture the diversity of human-generated queries, so relying on them alone narrows the query space. Sannigrahi et al. 2024 applies LLM synthetic query generation in a virtual-assistant setting, useful as a template for controlling query style and register. A related zero-shot idea, Gao et al. 2023 (HyDE), generates a hypothetical document from a query to improve retrieval without relevance labels — the inverse direction, but it reinforces the point that LLMs can bridge queries and documents in both directions. The practical warning across this route: generated queries are systematically “cleaner” and less diverse than human queries — mitigate by seeding with real query-log statistics (Kacprzak) and by generating multiple variants per intent.

Route Two-B: LLM relevance judging (synthetic qrels)

This is the route that most directly attacks your hardest link — producing graded qrels at scale — and it is the most active area in the review (11 papers, 2023–2025). The core idea: prompt an LLM with a (query, dataset) pair and have it output a graded relevance label, replacing or augmenting human assessors.

The agenda-setting paper is Faggioli et al. 2023 (Perspectives on LLMs for Relevance Judgment), which lays out a human–machine collaboration spectrum — from fully manual, through LLM-assisted, to fully automated judgments — and argues the right question is not “human or machine” but “how much to rely on the machine.” This spectrum is the correct mental model for designing NYC qrels. The empirical case that LLMs can judge well came from Thomas et al. 2024 (LLMs Accurately Predict Searcher Preferences) at Microsoft Bing, which calibrated an LLM and prompt against careful feedback from real searchers and showed the LLM’s labels can reach human quality. Because that study shipped no reusable software, Upadhyay et al. 2024 (UMBRELA) produced an open-source reproduction of the Bing relevance assessor — a concrete, runnable tool you could point at (query, NYC-dataset) pairs. On the query side of test-collection generation, Rahmani et al. 2024 (Synthetic Test Collections for Retrieval Evaluation) shows LLMs can generate both the queries and the judgments for a usable test collection, i.e., a fully synthetic benchmark, and studies how faithful the resulting system rankings are.

Several papers address the practical mechanics you will need. MacAvaney and Soldaini 2023 (One-Shot Labeling) tackles the “holes” problem — unjudged documents in an offline collection — by using an LLM to fill missing judgments, explicitly in the common setting where only one relevant document per query is known; this is exactly how you would extend a small human-judged seed to full-pool coverage. Rahmani et al. 2024 (JudgeBlender) shows that ensembling judgments from multiple LLMs, rather than relying on a single GPT-4, reduces intra-model bias (the tendency to favor systems built on the same model) and improves reliability. Dietz et al. 2025 (Principles and Guidelines for LLM Judges) and Arabzadeh et al. 2025 (Benchmarking LLM-based Relevance Judgment Methods) consolidate best practices and compare judging methods head-to-head — read these before finalizing a prompt.

The honest counterweight — and the reason no serious benchmark today is purely LLM-judged — comes from three papers you should read as a set. Upadhyay et al. 2024 (Large-Scale Study, TREC 2024 RAG Track) deployed manual and three LLM-assisted judging approaches in situ and correlated them, finding that LLM judgments preserve system rankings well but diverge from humans in absolute terms. Clarke and Dietz 2024 (LLM assessment still can’t replace human) directly rebuts the strong claim that UMBRELA-style judgments can fully replace humans in TREC-style evaluation, laying out practical and theoretical limitations. And Alaofi et al. 2024 (LLMs can be Fooled into Labelling a Document as Relevant) shows LLM judges are manipulable — keyword-stuffed or adversarial content (“best café near me”) can extract a spurious “relevant” label — a real vulnerability if any system under evaluation could game the judge. The synthesis: use LLMs for scale and for filling holes, keep a human-judged core for calibration and audit, ensemble judges to cut bias, and never let a single LLM judge be the sole ground truth.

Supporting route: dataset-retrieval methods and representation

These works are not about query/qrels construction per se, but they shape what your benchmark must be able to evaluate and how datasets should be represented to a judge. Brickley et al. 2019 (Google Dataset Search) is the most-cited work in this review and defines the production reality of open-web dataset search — metadata-driven, schema.org-based — which is the baseline any NYC benchmark should include as a lexical/metadata retriever. Paton et al. 2023 (Dataset Discovery and Exploration: A Survey) is the best single map of the whole field. On representation, Zhang and Balog 2018 (Ad Hoc Table Retrieval) established semantic-similarity methods for retrieving tabular data — directly relevant since most NYC Open Data assets are tables. Recent LLM-era work matters for the judge’s input: Chen et al. 2024 (Compact Data Snippets) shows how to summarize a large dataset into a snippet an assessor (human or LLM) can actually read; Zhang et al. 2026 (AutoDDG) auto-generates dataset descriptions with LLMs, useful when NYC metadata is sparse; Silva et al. 2024 improves dense retrieval for dataset search with LLM-augmented data; and Kayali et al. 2024 (Chorus) applies foundation models to unified data discovery. The takeaway for benchmark design: decide up front how a dataset is presented to the judge (metadata only? metadata + schema? + a data-sample snippet?), because that choice — not just the ranking model — determines the ceiling of achievable relevance-judgment quality. ACORDAR and Compact Data Snippets both argue for giving the judge the data, not only the description.

Open problems and what to track

Several questions remain genuinely unresolved and are worth watching as you build. Field-level relevance: DSEBench’s move from whole-dataset to field-level judgment suggests future qrels may need to mark which columns satisfy a query — more informative but far costlier to judge. How to present a dataset to a judge: there is no settled standard for how much data content (vs. metadata) a judge should see, yet ACORDAR shows this materially changes judgments. LLM-judge robustness and bias: the manipulability (Alaofi 2024) and intra-model favoritism (JudgeBlender) problems are actively researched but unsolved; ensembling helps but does not eliminate them. The human-replaceability boundary: whether — and for which query types — LLM judges can safely stand in for humans is exactly the open debate between the “accurately predict preferences” and “can’t replace human” camps. For NYC specifically, the biggest practical unknown is query provenance: whether you can obtain real portal logs will determine how much you must lean on synthetic query generation.
