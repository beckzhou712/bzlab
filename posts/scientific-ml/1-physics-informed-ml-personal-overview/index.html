Physics Informed Machine Learning: Personal Overview

11 Oct, 2026
|Edit page

This post is my understanding of the field after going through the Physics Informed Machine Learning lecture series by Steve Brunton (University of Washington).

What the field is

At bottom, ML is building data-driven models with optimization and regression techniques. Physics informed ML uses the same toolkit to build physical models. It asks two dual questions:

  • Using known physics to improve ML. Take physics that is known or partly known (symmetries, conservation laws, invariances, and so on) and embed it in the model. With the physics enforced, a model can learn from less training data and generalize better.
  • Using ML to discover new physics. Given measurements of galactic motion, plasmas, or biological systems, use ML methods to find physical models that people could not write down before because the systems are too complex. The output is ordinary or partial differential equations describing those systems.

One gold standard for whether a model is “physical” is parsimony: the model should be simple enough to generalize and to be interpreted. From Aristotle to Einstein, this standard has been in use for about two thousand years — everything should be made as simple as possible to describe the data, but not simpler. F=maF = maF=ma has only a few terms, and each has a simple physical meaning, so it is highly interpretable; it holds for a falling apple and for a moon landing, so it is highly generalizable.

Putting physics in: Problem, Data, Architecture, Loss, Optimization

Problem: what people can’t do, can’t be bothered to do, or can’t afford to do

First, make sure an ML model is actually needed. A few kinds of motivation:

  • start from a first-principles physical model and learn only the missing part, as in the closure problem;
  • surrogate modeling, where a cheap model stands in for an expensive simulation;
  • use automatic differentiability to plug the model into classical engineering design and optimization loops;
  • capture multiphysics interactions, for example by modeling the discrepancy.

Many problems come down to a dynamical system, a process governed by some differential equation. Both mathematically and in engineering practice, it is hard to predict a system’s long-term behavior deterministically. The alternative is to ask: given an initial condition, what is the probability that the system is at a given position at some future time?

Data: machines process data, humans understand it

Two kinds of physics are mainly useful at the data stage. One is data augmentation based on physical properties: symmetries correspond to invariances, which can be used directly to expand the data. The other is choosing the right coordinate system, where human expertise, physical knowledge, and intuition matter a great deal.

Data from different sources has different properties, so the model handles it differently. Simulation data is information-rich, high-resolution, and expensive, but physically more simplified; experimental data carries less information and may be cheap, but it contains the real details. Experiment remains the gold standard, and simulations can be improved against it. In practice multi-fidelity sources are common, with the expensive, scarce, high-fidelity data treated as ground truth. Interestingly, ML is close to a general-purpose data-processing framework: a single general algorithm can automatically stitch together data from simulations and experiments, across modalities, fidelities, and spatial and temporal resolutions. Note that training data may already carry the assumptions and biases of older models — use simulation data and you inherit the simulation’s assumptions.

Two points from this lecture stayed with me.

Small, subtle signals. Take the perihelion of Mercury. Newton’s second law explains 99.9% of Mercury’s motion; relativity, with gravitational effects and the curvature of spacetime, is there only to capture the last tiny bit. An ML model trained only to minimize error would almost certainly choose F=maF = maF=ma. Humans built an entire scientific process that lets us tell signal from noise in very small, very sparse information and, when it is signal, ask what mechanism explains the discrepancy.

The art of selective ignoring. When collecting data, ask: which parts of this do I actually want to model? Is the goal to discover a physical mechanism, or to get a model that works and fits the data better? Are the parts you set aside physical mechanisms you should attend to, or confounding factors? Throughout the history of science, humans have strategically chosen which parts of the data to ignore and which to attend to. A machine will not ignore any part of the data unless you guide it.

Finally, data need not be static, and in modern ML it often isn’t. So you can build a feedback loop: repeatedly query simulations and experiments of different fidelities to inform the model, which is far cheaper and more efficient than collecting everything in one pass.

Architecture: constraining the function space

Every discussion of architecture comes down to constraining the space of functions fff that can describe the input–output map. Mathematically, function space is nearly infinite-dimensional; choosing an architecture narrows it to a subset parameterized by free parameters θ\thetaθ, and training tunes θ\thetaθ within that subset to find the function we want. Some parameterizations let physics into the model more easily than others.

A few examples that come up later:

  • ResNet
  • DeepONet
  • Operator networks
  • SINDy
  • PINNs

There is a wonderful analogy here: progress in ML architectures is like the move from alchemy to chemistry. Alchemy runs on intuition and on watching what others do; you try a pile of things, and sometimes you turn stone into gold and sometimes you don’t. We want ML to become chemistry: applied with principles and organization, knowing which building blocks to use for which problem.

Progress in classical ML has ultimately been accelerated and validated by benchmark datasets and benchmark problems. Pushing these algorithms toward science, engineering, discovery, and design needs new benchmarks built on physical systems. One application is the digital twin: a living model of a complex system such as an aircraft, a factory floor, or a composite curing process, which should be as accurate as possible and keep updating as new data comes in.

Loss: physics as a penalty term

This is one of the easiest ways to bake physics into an ML model. On top of the data-fit loss, add a term that measures how badly the physics is violated:

L=Ldata+λ Lphysics\mathcal L = \mathcal L_{\text{data}} + \lambda\,\mathcal L_{\text{physics}}L=Ldata​+λLphysics​
  • PINN is the most direct example. Thanks to automatic differentiation, the partial derivatives of the network’s output with respect to its input are nearly free, so one can add a loss term that is literally the norm of the PDE, measuring at the data points how badly the governing equation is violated.
  • Lagrangian / Hamiltonian neural networks: when the system is known to conserve energy, have the network learn a Lagrangian or a Hamiltonian, then use the loss to require that it satisfy the Euler–Lagrange equations or Hamilton’s equations.
  • Promoting parsimony with norms: the 2-norm promotes low dimensionality, the 1-norm promotes sparsity.
  • Equivariance, invariance, and symmetry can be expressed in the loss as well.

Optimization: promote vs. enforce

Adding a physics term to the loss amounts to “suggesting” that the physics be satisfied. But the loss terms may fight each other: low model error and satisfied physics are sometimes opposing goals, and the physics term is never exactly zero. So physics in the loss is promoted, not enforced. Enforcing is optimization’s job.

The general recipe: first translate the “physics” into a mathematical expression (translation invariance, rotation invariance, energy conservation, and so on), then map it to a specific structure the model must have (satisfying a set of equality constraints, or lying on some matrix manifold), and finally design a custom optimization algorithm that solves the problem under that structural constraint.

  • Constrained SINDy: rather than adding the energy-conservation constraint equations to the loss, do constrained least squares, minimizing the model error while satisfying the constraints exactly.
  • Physics-informed DMD: constrain the model to a physics-informed matrix manifold; underneath is a Procrustes problem.

Almost any constraint you can think of is either linear or a manifold constraint. Linear constraints often have easy, exact closed-form solutions, and the Procrustes problem shows that submanifold constraints often have exact solutions too.

Everything in ML is ultimately an optimization problem: find the solution that minimizes the loss function. Optimization can often be taken for granted, since the basic routines are already very powerful, but certain constraints or losses often need a custom algorithm. Hyperparameters (such as λ\lambdaλ) can be chosen with a Pareto curve.

Coordinates: finding the right basis to simplify nonlinear dynamics

Suppose we have a high-dimensional state xxx (say, a video of a pendulum). An autoencoder learns an encoder φ\varphiφ and a decoder ψ\psiψ that map xxx to a latent space zzz, where zzz keeps only the minimum information needed to describe the system. The aim is for the dynamics x˙=f(x)\dot x = f(x)x˙=f(x) to have an efficient, simple representation in the latent space. In a sense, SVD / POD / PCA can be read as a shallow linear autoencoder: it decomposes a complex system containing low-dimensional patterns into a linear combination of a few modes that capture most of the variance. The deep autoencoder is its nonlinear generalization.

Two common approaches:

  • SINDy autoencoder: requires the dynamics of zzz to be a sparse linear combination of a library of candidate nonlinear terms. The loss has several parts: a reconstruction loss, SINDy’s L1L_1L1​ sparsity term, and extra terms that keep the time derivatives consistent across the sub-networks.
  • Deep Koopman autoencoder: asks for more: a coordinate system in which the dynamics become linear. Koopman operator theory backs the existence of such coordinates.

The community looking for linear representations splits into two philosophies. A constrictive autoencoder compresses the high-dimensional state into a low-dimensional latent space, models it linearly there, and decodes back, which is more interpretable. Lifting raises the state to a higher-dimensional zzz, models it with linear dynamics given by a matrix KKK, and maps back to xxx, much in the spirit of kernel methods in ML.

SINDy: finding sparse equations automatically

SINDy (sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics) uses sparse regression to discover equations from data. It extracts interpretable, generalizable dynamical-system models from time-series data, and its core assumption is that the true dynamics are simple: Newton’s second law, the seven terms of the Lorenz system, Navier–Stokes — each has only a handful of terms.

The method builds a library of candidate terms Θ(X)\Theta(X)Θ(X) and writes the time derivative as a sparse combination of its columns:

X˙=Θ(X) C\dot X = \Theta(X)\,CX˙=Θ(X)C

with CCC as sparse as possible. Extended to PDEs, it writes the time derivative of some quantity as a sparse combination of a library of “candidate partial derivatives and their nonlinear products.”

x→  φ  z⏟Encoderz˙=f(z)⏟SINDyz→  ψ  x^⏟Decoder\underbrace{x \xrightarrow{\;\varphi\;} z}_{\text{Encoder}} \qquad \underbrace{\dot z = f(z)}_{\text{SINDy}} \qquad \underbrace{z \xrightarrow{\;\psi\;} \hat x}_{\text{Decoder}}Encoderxφ​z​​SINDyz˙=f(z)​​Decoderzψ​x^​​

Data. SINDy approximates derivatives from the data. When the data is clean and sampled fast enough, a dynamical system can be identified from a very short stretch of time; when the sampling rate is low or the noise is high, it takes more data, a longer time, and denoising, such as regularized derivatives (total variation) or fitting a polynomial first and differentiating that. The weak formulation integrates both sides of the equation and handles larger noise by averaging. In experiments one can often design the experiment to excite different regions of parameter space or phase space, which is crucial for SINDy.

Coordinates. SINDy assumes the variables you measured are the right variables. If the coordinates are rotated or warped, a sparse representation may not be found, and getting this right depends heavily on domain knowledge. In high dimensions one can first get a low-dimensional basis from SVD / POD, or use the SINDy autoencoder from the previous section. When there are hidden variables, use time-delay embedding plus an SVD of the Hankel matrix (put simply: record the measurable variables at different times, arrange them into a matrix, and apply the SVD). In these coordinates the sparsest model is linear, which connects back to Koopman theory.

Library. SINDy is ultimately a generalized linear regression, and how the library is built determines what can be found.

  • Start with linear terms and add complexity step by step according to fit and prediction, while keeping an eye on the condition number of Θ\ThetaΘ.
  • Treat each system on its own terms; for a periodic system, for example, try a trigonometric basis.
  • Dynamics are often not a function of the state alone. External forcing, control inputs uuu, and bifurcation parameters can all be brought into the library by adding columns.
  • Rational-function dynamics: implicit SINDy multiplies both sides by the denominator, which turns the problem into finding a sparse vector in a null space (Θc=0\Theta c = 0Θc=0), but this is ill-posed under noise. SINDy-PI moves the candidate terms to the right-hand side one at a time and recovers the robustness of standard SINDy.
  • The library suffers from the curse of dimensionality. Remedies include SVD / PCA, deep autoencoders, and tensor trains. If other columns are nearly linearly dependent on the columns of the true model, sparse identification becomes very hard, so keep Θ\ThetaΘ as small as possible and collect richer transient data and more initial conditions.
  • Physical priors can constrain the library directly, for example by striking out terms that are incompatible with a symmetry.

Optimization. Plain least squares overfits and keeps too many terms. The usual choice in practice is sequential threshold least squares (STLSQ): run least squares, set coefficients below a threshold λ\lambdaλ to zero, run least squares again on the remaining terms, and iterate to convergence. Varying λ\lambdaλ gives a family of models of different sparsity, and empirically the good models sit at the “elbow” of the error–complexity Pareto curve. SR3 (sparse relaxed regularized regression) introduces an auxiliary variable www to take pressure off CCC: CCC can be as sparse as possible, while www stays close to CCC and takes care of the fit. Physics can be added in the optimization as well, such as equality constraints for energy conservation or stability constraints (stability-constrained SINDy). There is also abrupt-change SINDy, which updates the model quickly after a small change in the system and suits real-time control.

Discrepancy modeling: Galileo or Aristotle

Another way to use ML is to skip end-to-end learning: start from an imperfect model and focus the ML effort on the discrepancy, the part of the model that does not fit the data. The idealized model comes from first principles, and it is then augmented with the terms that are hard to model from first principles and with subtler physical effects (wind resistance, for example). There are two axes here. Unknown physics and disturbances are hard to write down from first principles and easy for ML to learn; known physics, such as constraints and energy conservation, is hard for ML to learn and has to come from first principles.

One example is Galileo’s falling bodies. Had Galileo actually collected data by dropping beach balls, basketballs, and golf balls instead of dense balls, he would very likely have arrived not at constant acceleration but at Aristotle’s model, in which the rate of fall depends on mass or density. Aristotle’s model dominated for more than a thousand years because it fits the data better. Galileo got to an idealized, universal model because he used carefully controlled experiments to separate out discrepancies such as drag.

This is also a reminder to think clearly when modeling with ML: do you want an idealized model that can be analyzed, learned from, and generalizes well, or a model that simply fits the data more accurately by brute force? It is the same question as the Mercury example and the art of selective ignoring above.

Writing mechanical structure into the network: HNN and LNN

A Hamiltonian system can be thought of as an energy-conserving, non-dissipative system; Noether’s theorem ties conserved quantities to symmetries. The naive approach has the network predict (q˙,p˙)(\dot q, \dot p)(q˙​,p˙​) directly. An HNN instead has the network learn the Hamiltonian H(q,p)H(q,p)H(q,p) and uses the loss to promote

q˙=∂H∂p,p˙=−∂H∂q\dot q = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p},\qquad \dot p = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q}q˙​=∂p∂H​,p˙​=−∂q∂H​

It amounts to a custom neural ODE with extra structure, with physics added in both the architecture and the loss. An LNN is similar: learn a Lagrangian L(q,q˙)L(q,\dot q)L(q,q˙​), take its partial derivatives, and use the loss to require that it satisfy the Euler–Lagrange equations. On mechanical systems like the double pendulum the improvement is large. Compared with an HNN, an LNN needs no canonical coordinates or conjugate momenta and works for any choice of q,q˙q,\dot qq,q˙​, so it is more flexible.

From ResNet to Neural ODE

The idea behind ResNet: rather than learn the whole map from xxx to yyy, copy the input straight across and model only the difference between the two, y=x+F(x)y = x + F(x)y=x+F(x). In a super-resolution task, for instance, most of the information in the output is already in the low-resolution input, and a relatively small residual FFF only has to capture the remaining fine features. This makes it possible to train much deeper networks without “forgetting” the input by the end of the network.

Applied to a dynamical system,

xk+1=xk+F(xk)x_{k+1} = x_k + F(x_k)xk+1​=xk​+F(xk​)

is almost exactly Euler integration, and Euler is a poor integration scheme. The idea of the Neural ODE is to model the differential equation itself, x˙=F(x)\dot x = F(x)x˙=F(x), and advance the state with a better integrator:

xk+1=xk+∫tktk+1F(x(τ)) dτx_{k+1} = x_k + \int_{t_k}^{t_{k+1}} F(x(\tau))\,d\tauxk+1​=xk​+∫tk​tk+1​​F(x(τ))dτ

Modeling in continuous time is more flexible: one can swap in better integrators or integrators with structure, and irregularly spaced data is fit better. A practical combination is to train a neural ODE on irregular data, integrate it to generate regularly spaced data, and hand that to DMD, SINDy, or symbolic regression. Those methods often do poorly on irregular data, while the neural ODE itself is powerful but not interpretable.

Learning operators: DeepONet and FNO

If an ODE / PDE is driven by some external forcing function, there is a map from the forcing function to the solution function, and that map is an operator. The universal approximation theorem for ordinary neural networks is about approximating functions from input to output; for most differential equations, we actually care about the solution operator, which maps an input function to an output function. Once learned, it gives the solution for a new forcing function without another large simulation.

DeepONet uses two networks: a branch net encodes the input function (its values at fixed sensor locations), a trunk net encodes the output location, and the two are combined to give the solution. The training data are numerical solutions obtained for sampled random forcing functions uuu. The paper shows that this split generalizes better and is more accurate than one large fully connected network, though the ODEs / PDEs it handles are relatively simple.

FNO has the same goal but uses the Fourier transform, learning how to solve the PDE in Fourier space. Fourier-based spectral methods are already a good way to solve problems in computational physics; put another way, the Fourier transform is a good physical coordinate system. More generally, a neural operator can be customized through its kernel KKK, and physics can go into the design of the kernel. FNO emphasizes discretization invariance: it learns a mesh-independent function-to-function map, so it can do zero-shot super-resolution. That said, prediction accuracy may change when the mesh changes, and performance on finer meshes still needs study. FNO suits periodic boundary conditions and may not suit complex geometries. The Laplace neural operator is one generalization of it.

Representing the field itself: NIF

Neural Implicit Flow splits complex spatiotemporal dynamics into two parts: a shape net, much like a PINN’s network, represents the output field as a function of space; a parameter net modulates the shape net with exogenous variables such as time, Mach number, Reynolds number, and control inputs. It is mainly a data-driven field representation (a surrogate) and does not explicitly discover the equations of the dynamics. Its main advantage is handling arbitrary meshes and resolutions. In applications, it can compress large turbulence datasets (storing only the network weights), and it can be combined with sparse sensors: feed point-sensor readings into the parameter net and reconstruct the whole flow field at any location.

Searching for symbolic expressions: PySR

PySR does symbolic regression, and the method is genetic programming: over a population of candidate equations it repeatedly applies mutation (changing one term in an equation), crossover (two equations swapping terms), simplification (reducing and removing redundancy), and constant optimization. At the end it picks, from the accuracy–complexity Pareto front, the one that is “as simple as possible, but not simpler.” It is a direct expression of interpretability and the parsimony principle.

PINN

The core idea of a PINN is simple: require the network both to predict accurately and to obey known physical laws as far as it can. Technically, the PDE is written in residual form and minimizing that residual becomes the physics loss. Because the PDE itself is the supervision signal, a PINN does not need ground truth at every location to train the physics loss there, so it is good at estimating a whole flow field from sparse sensor data.

Its limitations come from the same design. The physics is a soft constraint, and the residual is almost never exactly zero; on top of that, the data loss and the physics loss can conflict, so once the physics term is added the pure fit may be worse than with the data loss alone. Here too a Pareto front can be used to weigh the trade-off. Improvements on the optimization side come in two kinds:

  • Curriculum regularization: gradually increase the difficulty of the physics constraint, L=Ldata+λ(t) Lphysics\mathcal L = \mathcal L_{\text{data}} + \lambda(t)\,\mathcal L_{\text{physics}}L=Ldata​+λ(t)Lphysics​;
  • Sequence-to-sequence learning: split long-horizon dynamics into shorter time windows, which are easier to optimize locally, at the cost of errors accumulating step by step.

Extensions of PINN include fractional PINN, which handles integer-order terms with automatic differentiation and fractional-order and integral terms with numerical discretization, and Δ-PINN, which brings in geometry through Laplace–Beltrami eigenfunctions (for a heart or a wing, say).

Recap & Summary

For me, physics informed ML is exciting because it uses scientific knowledge to intervene in a black-box model in a targeted way (though the model is not always a black box), and can even go the other way and learn science from the black-box model. It requires scientific knowledge that can be stated in the language of mathematics, which not every scientific field offers but physics and engineering do. It rests on general-purpose ML techniques and general-purpose science, so it is general in method and vertical in application, and it has proven effective in many applications.

Its implicit view of the world also gives me a lot to think about, the parsimony principle for one. “As simple as possible, but not simpler”: for physics and mathematics, which pursue precision, what a vague thing to say. Yet when you are inside the vast function space of SINDy or PySR, pick out a family of plausible-looking expressions at the elbow of the error–complexity Pareto curve (with no accepted theory telling you how to balance complexity against error), and then find that they fit well and generalize well too, you start to wonder: could the same method produce Newton’s second law, or the Navier–Stokes equations? You start to ask whether science could come apart from human understanding and discover new physics purely at the level of technique, like an assembly line.

For now, it can’t. Re-identifying known equations from data has been done, but only because people chose the right variables and candidate terms beforehand. Take coordinates: SINDy needs good coordinates, and judging that usually takes physical knowledge; a method like FNO can also be read as borrowing the idea of Fourier spectral methods from physics, treating Fourier space as the physical coordinate system. There are, of course, methods that learn coordinates, such as autoencoders and Koopman, but the latent coordinates they learn do not necessarily have a clear physical meaning, and the simplicity of the dynamics in them also depends on how the architecture and the loss function are designed.

The coordinate example points at the part of science from which human understanding cannot be stripped away, at least for now. More broadly, meaning derives from purpose, or perhaps the two are born together. Function space holds an enormous number of functions, and we use the loss function (that is, the purpose) to pick out the one that fits the task. The other functions were there all along; they were just never selected by a human-scale purpose, and so they carry no human-scale meaning. Without a purpose there is no optimization, no strategy, no winner. In this sense science has always been an art of selective ignoring: take from the whole space the part that humans can give meaning to, and ignore the rest. A machine, though, has no purpose and understands no meaning. It can draw the sparsity–error curve but cannot decide which part of the curve deserves to be chosen as the physical model; nor will it judge for itself whether the unexplained variance is signal or noise. To this day, science still needs people to interpret it, and models still need people to guide them.

Last, a bit of personal brainstorming. A model can only carry out the loss function a human gives it — so could the model learn the loss function itself? Take “driving well”: we probably couldn’t write an explicit loss function for that either. People are already working in this direction; inverse reinforcement learning infers the reward from human demonstrations, and driving is its classic example. But learning a loss function still needs a loss function of its own. Perhaps one could specify only the most basic physical constraints and error measures, and leave the rest for the model to explore.
